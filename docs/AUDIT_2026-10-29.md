# Audit: input versus output across story, narration, storyboard, pictures, video and packaging

Written 2026-10-29. Audience: the owner and whoever builds the next batches. Goal of the audit: find out what stops this app from making videos that earn views, watch time and shares on YouTube in 2026, and list what to change, with special attention to what we send to the chat model (Gemma, served through the gateway as `gemma4-12b-qat-uncensored-hauhaucs-balanced`) and to the image model (Qwen Image 2.1).

## 0. Summary

**Verdict.** The machine works end to end and the pictures are better than most automated channels. What it produces is still a *slideshow of generic mood pictures over a padded script*, not a story told by a camera. The cause is not the image model or the voice. It is **what the chat model is given and what it is asked to do**:

1. **Gemma writes from a 300-word summary, not from the story.** The source article of the latest film (40 paragraphs) holds about 30 vivid, concrete details (a meat cleaver, Dharavi, an orange orchard in Nagpur, a wife and a one-year-old daughter left behind, calls to the family from a keypad phone, a six-hour wait outside a building, a terrace room, the murder house now split into four homes). The narration uses about six of them. The rest of the script is filler such as "सोचिए", "एक बहुत बड़ा सच सामने आया", "यह एक बहुत ही interesting job है". The pipeline withholds the source passages on purpose (leak rule) and passes only a compressed fact list; that compression throws away the material a storyteller needs.
2. **The storyboard illustrates words, not events.** Because the narration is vague, the scenes are vague: "Geeta Suri's presence", "Montu's breathing", "the shadow of a nearby object shifts", "hands gripping a clay pot" for the idea of a caretaker job. The director plans only the *camera* (size, angle, move), never the *story event* each shot must show.
3. **The world is tiny.** Across nine stored films the number of distinct places is 2 to 6 for 36 to 71 scenes; one or two places hold 50 to 83% of all scenes; 63 to 85% of scenes in the crime films contain a person, and one protagonist is in 30 to 50% of them. This is exactly the "repetitive scenes" the owner sees.
4. **Characters are not true to the story.** The 62-year-old fugitive is drawn about 38 in every scene, including 1991 when he was 27. An 18-year-old son is drawn as a boy of about 9. A murder victim is drawn smiling with closed eyes. Seven different police officers are one face. A police officer holds a smartphone in the victim's 1991 home when the story is about a keypad phone traced to Ludhiana.
5. **The image prompt spends half its words on text that is the same in every scene** (42 to 56% identical sentences), contains a "must not appear" list the model cannot honour at cfg 1, and states things that contradict each other (a "quiet street" with "crowds" excluded, and a line "Background populated with bystanders" added to the same prompt).
6. **The thumbnail, the strongest click lever, is the weakest output.** The latest thumbnail shows two *women's* faces for a story about one man, a map with garbled place names ("Hablia", "Monini") and a photoreal look that does not match the video.
7. **The video has no sound design** (no music, ambience or effects), small thin captions, scenes up to 33 s long on one still, and a 60-second opening monologue.
8. **The scorecards do not see any of this.** The latest plan was approved by auto-run with `opening_strength 100`, `narrative_clarity 100`, `originality 100` and `visual_relevance 92`. The checks measure format (schema, hashes, repeats), not storytelling.
9. **Platform risk in 2026.** YouTube's July 2026 policy clarification names *repetitive or template-based content* as non-monetisable, even if AI-made. A pipeline that outputs the same structure, same look, same phrases for every video sits close to that line unless variety and original input are built in.
10. **Legal and trust risk.** The narration states "उसने Geeta Suri को मार दिया" as fact; the source says "allegedly". Real private victims are named and drawn.

**Viewer and audience findings (section 14, added later):** information density halves by the middle of every film (new-word share 97% at the start, 29 to 33% in the last three minutes), the film's promised answer arrives last and thin, only 0 or 1 new place or subject appears per 30 seconds for minutes at a time, and the Indian audience personas (young Hindi-first, parents and seniors, regional viewers, women, sceptics) are served poorly: regional cues are almost absent, numbers are spoken in English, jargon is unexplained, names do not match the cast.

**What to build first (details in section 7):** (a) a *Material Pack* step that extracts 30 to 40 paraphrased concrete details from the source passages and feeds them to blueprint, narration and storyboard; (b) a *visual events ledger* so every scene shows a distinct story event, with a place budget and character life stages; (c) a three-pass narration (free-prose draft, editor, tagger) at proper temperatures with a code-enforced phrase and question budget; (d) a rewritten image prompt that is about 100 to 140 words of story-specific prose; (e) a thumbnail and title that are generated and scored as a pair; (f) a sound bed, bigger captions and a hard scene-length cap; (g) an automatic audit scorecard (section 8) that runs after every film so improvement is measured, not felt.

## 3. Findings, stage by stage

### 3.1 Story selection and intake (Phase 1 to Phase 2 bridge)

- **Compression of the source.** `story2/intake.py` builds `facts_text` from: premise, a 100-word overview, people, 8 to 12 chronology lines, tension, stakes, turning point, outcome, claim *texts only* and gaps. For the newest film that is about 350 words from a 1,900-word article. The passages themselves go only to the leak checker (`evidence.passages`). Every later model call (blueprint, narration, registry, director, storyboard) sees only this summary.
- **What was lost in the newest film** (all in the source, none in the facts): the cleaver attack and the teenage son's scuffle; the hospital; that he wandered the streets until dawn and took a train; Dharavi daily-wage work; learning of the police raid on his home and running; the orange orchard; hotels in Goa; the businessman who offered a garment-mill job; the forged Aadhaar under a new name; his wife and one-year-old daughter, whom police questioned; the era (no mobile phones, no CCTV, no tenant verification); that the break came from *calls to his own family* from one number, found by checking over a dozen relatives' numbers, and that the SIM was in a keypad phone; the six-hour surveillance, the beedi, the shop, the keypad phone taken out of the pocket as confirmation, the shout; his barsati on the terrace and Rs 15,000 pay; and the closing image, the murder house sold and turned into four homes. The film's own central question ("what finally gave him away") is **never answered** in the narration: it asks "क्या आप अंदाज़ा लगा सकते हैं कि यह phone number कहाँ से आया?" and moves on.
- **Same pattern in the Surat film:** the source explains a two-app mechanism (a "victim app" and an "admin app" that streams OTPs live), a Rs 15,000 monthly subscription (a crime-as-a-service model), the list of impersonated brands (RTO challan, SBI, PNB, PM Kisan, BigBasket), and a funnel (121 apps, 21,672 phones, 2,928 fully compromised, 54,094 transactions). The narration keeps "121 APKs" and the Rs 64 crore and fills the rest with "इतना बड़ा network", "empire", "mastermind".
- **Story scoring ignores the channel.** `story/settings.py` weights hook 25, narrative 20, payoff 20, emotion 15, standalone 20. Nothing scores *visual richness* (can it be shown?), *packaging* (is there a title and thumbnail that would work?), *audience demand or novelty*, or *safety* (private victims, minors, allegations).
- **Source dependence.** All stored films retell one news article or one book chapter. That is the situation YouTube's reused/template rules look at hardest (section 6).

### 3.2 Blueprint (Gemma call 1)

Measured on the newest film:

- The suspense plan fields exist and are used, which is good. Weak points: `false_answer` ("master of disguise or legendary thief") is a straw man the film never builds; `dark_moment` is a fact, not a moment of doubt.
- Beat 1 is 61 s of narration and gives away the climax (he turns, he is caught), then rewinds: the cold open consumes the payoff. A cold open should show the question, not the answer.
- `next_hook` is written in English and is **appended verbatim as the last narration line** (`story2/planner.py:321`), so the video ends on a full English sentence in a Hindi voice, and it is a teaser for a story that does not exist. Last spoken line of the newest film: "If a man can hide for thirty-five years just by making one assumption, what happens when the smallest clue—a single phone number—changes everything?".
- `chapter_title`, `music_brief`, `series_placement`, `indian_hook` are generated and mostly unused downstream (no chapters, no music).
- Names: the blueprint, narration and pictures mix "Chavi Lal", "Chabbi Lal", "Chhabbi Lal". The source itself is inconsistent; nothing decides one spelling for the voice and one for the screen.

### 3.3 Narration (Gemma call 2): what it receives and what it returns

**Inputs per beat** (`planner.make_narration`): `NARRATE` system prompt (about 1,900 words of rules), the facts block, blueprint summary, suspense plan, outline, last 3,500 characters of earlier sentences, and "purpose, takeaway, facts" of the beat (2 to 3 facts). Temperature is the global 0.2 (`story/settings.py`). The reply must be strict JSON with Devanagari text, emotion and pause per sentence, one beat per call.

**Measured output** (nine films):

| Measure | Range | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Questions as share of sentences | 7 to 17% in eight films, 1% in the 1987 crash film (newest 15%) | One in seven sentences is a question to the viewer |
| "सोचिए / क्या आप सोच सकते हैं / अंदाज़ा लगा" | 5 to 7 per film | Same device every beat |
| Stock openers ("मैं आपको...", "यहाँ मुझे सबसे ज़्यादा हैरानी", "चलिए मैं...") | 3 to 9 per film | Copied from the prompt's own examples |
| "twist / उलझन / यहाँ एक सवाल / गड़बड़" | 0 to 2 | Same |
| Beats ending on a question | 5 of 6, 6 of 7, 6 of 7 in crime films | The prompt says "not at the end of every part"; the check does not enforce it |
| Sentences tagged neutral or clear | 87 to 100% (older film 62%) | A flat read for 8 minutes |
| English words | 12 to 27% of words | Fine for Hinglish, but many are filler ("interesting", "simple", "massive") |

**Defects found on the page (newest film):**

1. *Padding and abstraction.* "यहाँ एक बहुत बड़ा सवाल आता है." "सच तो यह है कि उसने एक बहुत ही simple assumption बना लिया था." "यह एक बहुत ही interesting job है." About a third of the sentences carry no new fact.
2. *Euphemism where the story needs a concrete image.* The murder is "उस रात, एक खामोश मंजर हुआ" (a silent scene happened). The attack, the cleaver, the son's fight are absent. Fear of violence in the prompt (and the distress rule) pushes the model to blur, which kills the stakes.
3. *Cliché vocabulary:* ghost, shadow, invisible, mystery, empire, mastermind, genius, "iceberg की नोक", "जाल बिछा". These appear in all crime films.
4. *Invented atmosphere instead of researched atmosphere:* "शांत शाम", "सन्नाटा", "ख़ामोशी" repeat, while real details (the era without phones, the 6-hour watch, the beedi, the corner shop) are unused.
5. *Unanswered promise* (the phone number) and a **wrong ending** (the English teaser). The closing image the source offers (the house rebuilt as four homes) is unused.
6. *Allegation as fact:* "उसने Geeta Suri को मार दिया", "Rohit ... mastermind". The source says police alleged this. No attribution anywhere ("पुलिस के मुताबिक").
7. *Glamourising the offender:* "genius", "empire", `happy` tag on the arrest of an 18-year-old, which also breaks the prompt's own "happy only for relief".
8. *First person that adds nothing.* The rule "use मैं at least three times" produces "यहाँ मैं देख सकता हूँ कि", "मैं चाहता हूँ कि आप इस विशालता को महसूस करें" (announces a feeling instead of creating it).
9. *Overlong first beat* (61 s) and long beats of 80 to 104 s with no visual or tonal break in the script.
10. *Dark-matter film (explainer):* a good first line, then repeated "सोचिए", no concrete analogy that stays (the "crowded room whisper" analogy is told, then dropped), and numbers like "two hundred GeV per c squared" said aloud with no meaning attached.

**Root causes (Gemma side):**

- *Input starvation* (3.1): a model asked to "show before you explain, with something they can see, hear or smell" has nothing true to show, so it invents mood.
- *Temperature 0.2 for creative writing.* Low temperature maximises the most likely phrasing, which for Hinglish storytelling is the stock phrasing. All calls share one temperature, whether they write prose, plan shots or return JSON.
- *Prompt examples are copied.* The prompt quotes "चलिए मैं आपको दिखाता हूँ", "यहाँ मुझे सबसे ज़्यादा हैरानी हुई", "पर यहाँ एक गड़बड़ थी", "सोचिए आप वहाँ खड़े हैं". Small models copy examples. The repeated phrases in the output are the examples.
- *Rules contradict each other.* "Open a small question every 15 to 20 seconds" plus "speak to the viewer" plus "use मैं three times" plus "do not end every part with a question" cannot all be satisfied; the model satisfies the first and the third.
- *Prose is written inside JSON, in Devanagari, with emotion tags, under 20 rules.* Writing quality is the first thing to drop when a small model juggles format. The checks (`repeated_sentences`, `overused_openers`, romanised Hindi) can only reject; they cannot supply the missing content, and the last attempt is always accepted.
- *Each beat sees only 2 to 3 facts and the last 3,500 characters.* It does not know what concrete material is still unused, so late beats repeat earlier ones in new words.

### 3.4 Characters, places, props (registry)

- *Ages are not timeline-aware.* The registry prompt says "give each an age range" with no story timeline. The newest film's protagonist is "45 years old" in every prompt; the story needs 27 (1991) and 62 (now). A person who changes over decades needs life-stage variants.
- *Ensemble collapse.* Seven officers become "Police Officer" (one face, 18 scenes). The real story has a sub-inspector, constables and a senior officer; the viewer never sees a team.
- *Montu* (18 in the source) is drawn as a young boy; *Geeta* is drawn smiling with closed eyes while the narration says she was killed.
- *Places: 6 for the film, of which Ludhiana Street holds 28 scenes and The Suri Home 24.* The registry prompt asks for "at least 4", the cap is "8 to 14 assets", and props are limited to 3. The story needs 15 to 20 places (a curtain shop, the house at night, the hospital, a railway platform, Dharavi lanes, an orchard, a Goa hotel kitchen, a mill, a roadside paan shop, a rooftop room, a police office with phone records, a car, the court). Props that carry the plot (cleaver, keypad phone, blurred photo, forged ID) are valuable and are being dropped by the "at most three" rule.
- *Wrong place for an event.* A police officer examines the keypad phone "in The Suri Home" (scenes 41 to 48, 7 scenes) because the only indoor place on the list was the Suri home.
- *Real people.* Real, named private victims (Geeta Suri, Montu, the Delhi retired scientist, a named police team) are drawn as faces. See section 6.

### 3.5 Director and storyboard (Gemma calls 3 and 4)

**Director** (`story2/director.py`): it plans emotion, place and shots per segment, with checks on person share (<= 45%), hold share (<= 15%), size variety. Good. What it does not plan: **which story event each shot shows.** `focus` is free text ("Verma's face, half-shadowed"), and the storyboard then has to invent the event.

**Storyboard** (`story2/storyboard.py`, `SCENES` prompt): inputs are the facts summary, style, asset list, story map (first 90 characters of each segment), shot list, the next segment's first sentence, the last three scenes (type, distance, angle, subject) and the segment's sentences. It must return about 40 fields per scene.

Measured and seen:

- **Words are illustrated.** Examples from the newest film: "Geeta Suri's presence" (#8), "Montu's breathing" (#9, #13), "shadow of a nearby object shifts" (#14), "the mystery of his journey: a dusty road" (#22), "the essence of his job: hands gripping a clay pot" (#36), a quiet street for "the 35-year silence is broken" (#47), a transformer close-up for "how calm the evening must have been" (#52). The prompt's own rule "do not illustrate the words" is followed in form (an `idea` and a `translation` field) but not in effect, because there is no story event to translate *into*.
- **The same motif nine times.** The transformer from the opening (a good plant) appears in at least nine scenes (#1, 6, 28, 35, 40, 50, 51, 52, 58). A motif needs a budget.
- **Long holds.** Scene lengths average 6.5 to 8.7 s; maximum 33 s (newest film: 12 scenes over 12 s; `pl-52b99e91`: 14). The prompt says 3 to 7 s; the duration check only warns, and auto-run approves with warnings.
- **Built cards and invented text.** Dark matter: "99.5% PROBABILITY" and "1 MILE DEEP" cards (thin but correct). Surat film: "FAKE" and "1930" appear on an office whiteboard in the scammer's "hub", which is also a generic corporate office with ten monitors for an 18-year-old dropout who worked from his own devices. The 35-year film: a map where a man sits on India with unreadable glyphs (#23).
- **Voice-tag side effects.** The storyboard also chooses `voice_tag` per scene and `emotional_effect`. They are mostly "neutral", "curious", "softly".
- **Overloaded schema.** Fields such as `style`, `fixed_by_facts`, `creative_choices`, `needs_asset`, `fallback`, `use_ref_assets` take the model's attention. The `style` field is literally the word "style" in 43 of 59, 50 of 53, 63 of 65 and 49 of 59 scenes in four films; it carries no information.

### 3.6 Image prompts (Qwen Image 2.1)

The compiled prompt of scene 1 (309 words), in order: Shot (lens, angle), Visual style (45 words), Main subject plus full appearance, Action, Environment (with "Background populated with bystanders, movement and activity"), Composition ("The subject sits centre"), Lighting, Continuity, Aspect ratio and framing (40 words, including captions advice), Text (blank signs), Constraints (list of "must be clearly visible", "must not appear", "exactly 1 person", "no duplicates", "no floating objects, platforms or symbols, no anime or cartoon faces, no extra people").

Findings:

- **Half the words are boilerplate.** Computed over each film, 42 to 56% of prompt words come from sentences identical in at least 40% of scenes (style, framing, text, constraints, cast). The story-specific part is about 120 to 160 words. A long identical tail dilutes the part that differs, and pictures converge.
- **Negations in a model that does not use a negative prompt.** Qwen runs at cfg 1 here (`docs/APP_GUIDE.md`: "negative prompt ignored"). All prompts in all films carry "must not appear / no X" phrases (100% of 224 checked). Text encoders attend to the named concept, so "no crowds", "no anime", "no extra people" can *introduce* those concepts. The pictures show it: scene 1 and #3 are full of passers-by although "crowds" is excluded.
- **Contradictions inside one prompt.** "Background populated with bystanders" is added for any public place (`imgprompts._PUBLIC_RX`) while `exclusions` contains "crowds" (6 of 30 such scenes in the newest film), and the atmosphere says "quiet". "Close shot, 85mm portrait lens, shallow depth of field" is paired with "establishing" type and a background that must show the place.
- **Mis-assembled subject.** `Main subject: Ludhiana Street. Appearance of Chavi Lal Verma: ...` (the scene's `subject` was the place, the description is the person's). This confuses which thing is the focus.
- **"These must be clearly visible: Verma is in Ludhiana Street; He is looking at a transformer; Transformer."** These are facts, not visible things; they repeat the action and waste attention.
- **Mood by label.** "Atmosphere: quiet, dusty, still" and "Feeling of the moment: hope. Show it with eyes lifted and a faint steady smile" were applied to a police officer examining a clue and, in the murder scenes, to the victim. There is no guard that maps feelings to what is appropriate for the person and moment.
- **Unconnected detail.** "Footwear hidden" rules are sound, but the prompt still names the age wrongly (45 for a 62-year-old; 27 in 1991).
- **Format.** The Qwen text encoder is a language model and is generally best served by a natural paragraph that leads with who is doing what, then where, then camera and light. The labelled-section format ("Shot:", "Main subject:", "Constraints:") reads like a form. This is a hypothesis; it must be settled by a bench on our own prompts using the existing vision scorer (section 8).

### 3.7 Pictures

- Look: a coherent painterly film-still style; faces are readable and expressive. This is a real strength.
- Check scores (`refscore.look`): mean 71 (newest), 79, 77.5, 77, 67 across five films; 10 to 23 redraws per film; 22 to 117 s per image.
- **What the scorer flags** is mostly cosmetic ("extra pedestrians", "shop on the left competes"). It does not flag the big problems above because it compares picture to *prompt*, and the prompt is already wrong (age 45, smiling victim, officer in the wrong house).
- **Consistency:** the protagonist's face is broadly the same man across scenes (cast sheets and generate mode work reasonably) but is the same ~38-year-old; officers are interchangeable; other characters shift between scenes. `scene_pictures.mode` is `edit` for the newest film but the stored prompt of the first scene was generated plain with no reference attached (`references: []`, three `missing_references`), so face lock from reference sheets was not exercised. The owner's parked item "test edit mode against plain drawing for face consistency" is still the open question.
- **Variety by contact sheet:** (newest film) head-and-shoulders portrait of the protagonist in a street fills about 20 of 59 tiles; police-officer portrait in a room about 12; the same seated or standing "man in cream kurta" frame recurs. (Surat film) the same sofa/blue-shirt man appears 10 times in 28 tiles. (Dark matter) strong atmospheric stills, but a tunnel with a tank repeats and "crystal" metaphors appear although the registry rule bans symbols.
- **Time:** about 2h20m per 6-minute film on the single GPU, of which pictures are about 95 minutes including redraws.

### 3.8 Voice and captions

- Voice engine is Maya (`Siddhant`), per-sentence tags set by the storyboard and then by `voicetags`. In the newest film 99% of sentences carry emotion `neutral` or `clear`; tags are varied but small. Not judged by ear.
- The finished video is played at **1.25x**. Maya at about 148 words per minute becomes about 185, fast for an explanatory story in Hindi on a phone. This was the owner's decision; it should be tested for retention, because speed hides padding but also removes the pauses that make a reveal land.
- **Captions:** one caption per sentence, thin white text with a thin outline at the bottom (see frames at 30 s and 90 s). Fine on a desktop, small on a phone, and every caption is a full sentence. Modern retention practice for mobile is short phrase captions (3 to 6 words), larger and bolder, with emphasis on the key word.

### 3.9 Video assembly

- Moves come from the director (eased push, pull, pan, tilt). This is better than static slides.
- **No music, no ambience, no effects.** `blueprint.music_brief` is written and never used. A silent bed under a voice is a strong "AI slideshow" signal and removes the mood changes that carry a story.
- **No title or opening card, no chapters, no end screen or outro**, no pattern interrupts (zoom cuts, whooshes, text callouts) beyond the camera drift.
- **Motion clips (Wan 2.2)** exist (`story3/clips.py`, off by default). Two clips in one film. This is the right tool for a handful of key moments, not for all scenes.
- Draft is 1080p, 30 fps, no loudness normalisation recorded.

### 3.10 Thumbnail, title, metadata

- Thumbnail of the newest film: two enlarged *female* faces (the film is about one man; the faces were generated from an unspecified "South Asian adult" in the boost prompt), a map with misspelled nonsense labels, a different look from the video (photoreal against painterly), three competing areas. The vision check only reads the headline back (`text_ok: true`), so the garbled map text passed.
- Headline "Hidden for 35 Years?" is acceptable but generic. The story's real hook is more specific: a man caught because of a *phone call to his own family*, or the shout of his own name after 35 years.
- No title options, description, pinned comment, hashtags, chapters (the blueprint has them), upload captions (`.srt`) or end-screen plan. Without these the video relies on one title only.

### 3.11 Quality control

- Editorial scores are computed from checks that cannot see the story (`story2/editorial.py`). The newest film was approved with 63 warnings, `opening_strength 100`, `originality 100`, `narrative_clarity 100`.
- Auto-run is built to *never stop on style* and accepts last attempts. That is good for finishing a job and bad as a quality gate; nothing downstream blocks a mediocre film.

## 4. What "world class" looks like for this product (design principles)

These are the principles the changes below follow.

1. **Story first, camera second.** Decide the 25 to 40 things the viewer must *see* (events, objects, places, faces) before writing the script and before choosing a lens. Every scene is one of those events or a deliberate breath.
2. **Specific beats general, always.** One checked detail ("he kept the phone off") beats three mood sentences. Information density is a measurable property of a script.
3. **Show the question, answer it, then open a bigger one.** Open loops are limited and must close. A film that asks the viewer to guess something and never says it fails the contract.
4. **A believable world.** Many places, an ensemble of recognisable people with ages and roles that match the timeline, hero objects, and a small number of recurring motifs with a budget.
5. **Variety is a product feature, not an accident.** Within a film (scene length, place, subject, size, angle) and across films (structure, palette, music, opening form), because both viewers and YouTube's template detector punish sameness.
6. **Honest and safe.** Allegations are attributed, minors and private victims are protected, distress is handled with restraint, synthetic realism is disclosed where required.
7. **Packaging is part of the story.** Title, thumbnail, first 15 seconds and chapters are designed together from the same hook and tested.
8. **Every change is measured.** A fixed scorecard, a bench of frozen test stories, and human spot checks, so that "better" can be shown.

## 6. 2026 platform, policy and audience

Findings from the web checks (secondary sources unless stated; verify on YouTube's own policy pages):

- **Inauthentic content.** The rule was renamed from "repetitious content" in July 2025. A TechCrunch report dated 2026-07-20 states that YouTube's clarified policy lists three kinds of non-monetisable "inauthentic content": (1) repetitive or template-based content, generic videos that look alike, whether AI, CGI or templates; (2) off-putting or distressing content designed to upset or manipulate viewers; (3) AI personas discussing sensitive topics such as health and finance. Trade coverage adds that review can look at the whole channel. Implication for us: (1) is the standing risk of a pipeline whose every film has the same structure, the same style, the same phrases and the same captions; (2) applies to graphic reconstructions of murders and to "shocking" thumbnails; (3) is not our case unless a narrator persona is added to finance or health stories (the roadmap's "narrator avatar").
- **Synthetic-content disclosure.** Creators must disclose realistic altered or synthetic content (realistic people, altered footage of real events or places, realistic generated scenes). Clearly unrealistic or animated content is exempt. Our pictures are painterly and stylised, so disclosure is probably not required for most scenes, but *realistic-looking reconstructions of real crimes with real named victims* are a grey zone; add a checklist step and, when in doubt, disclose.
- **Retention in 2026.** Sources agree the first 30 seconds decide most drop-off; one 2026 analysis reports long-form views up and average view duration down, which means more competition for attention per video. Figures vary widely between blogs; treat benchmarks as rough. Practical consequences: a strong first line and picture within 3 seconds, the promise stated by about 20 seconds, a visible change at least every 4 to 5 seconds, a re-hook near the middle, and an ending that pays off the opening question.
- **Audience.** India is mobile-first and often sound-on but captions are heavily used. Text on screen and captions must be legible at phone size; thumbnails must work at about 170 by 95 pixels.

### P1: the Material Pack and the three-pass narration (M to L; biggest quality gain)

**M1. Material Pack** (`story2/material.py`, new; runs after story approval, before the blueprint).
- Input: the story's own source passages (`evidence.passages`) and the facts. Output, stored on the plan: 30 to 40 items, each `{id, kind, detail, when, where, who, visual_potential 0-3, emotional_weight 0-3, role}` where `kind` is one of: action, object, place, person-trait, number, sensory, irony, cause-chain, turning-clue, aftermath.
- Rule for the model: paraphrase, never quote, no source, author or brand mention; keep numbers exact; mark items as `alleged` when the source attributes them. The existing leak gate (`story2/leak.py`) and overlap check run on the pack, so the "nothing carried forward" rule stays intact. Because the pack is paraphrase and facts, not source wording, it satisfies the owner rule while giving the writer real material.
- Add a *chain of discovery* item list for investigation stories (what clue led to what), because the whole viewer question is usually "how did they find out".
- The blueprint, each narration beat, the registry and the storyboard all receive the relevant slice. Each beat lists the item ids it must use; the narration check fails a beat that uses fewer than 70% of them and sends back the missing ones by name.
- Verify: for the newest film, at least 20 of the 30 vivid details appear in the narration; the phone-number chain is answered.

**M2. Narration v2** (`story2/planner.py`, `story2/prompts.py`):
- Pass 1 (draft) prompt in plain prose: a storyteller persona *without* the rule list; the beat's ledger items; what the viewer should feel at the end of the beat; a hard budget of at most one question per eight sentences and at most four in the whole video; "start in the middle of a concrete moment".
- Pass 2 (editor): deletes sentences with no new information, replaces any phrase from a **banned list kept in code** (not shown as examples): "सोचिए", "क्या आप सोच सकते हैं", "यहाँ मुझे सबसे ज़्यादा हैरानी", "मैं आपको ... चाहता हूँ", "बहुत बड़ा सच", "twist", "mystery", "empire", "mastermind", "ghost", "interesting", "simple". Keep the list in `planner.py` and grow it from audit results.
- Pass 3 (tagger): JSON only.
- First person: do not demand a count. Let the narrator be a point of view that comments sparingly (two to four times in a video) and with opinion, not announcements.
- Opening: at most 15 s of cold open that *poses* the question with one concrete image; title card; promise by 25 s.
- Ending: payoff line that answers the opening question; one closing image; a comment question to the viewer ("आपके हिसाब से...?"), then a soft series line only when real.
- Best-of-two for the opening and the last beat with a judge pass using a short rubric (specificity, new information, no cliché, answers the question); the loser is kept for the editor.
- Allow beat length 20 to 45 s and make beats cut at a visual change; no beat over 60 s.
- Verify with the audit metrics in section 8 on the three bench stories.

**M3. Automatic Hindi quality gate (no human step, owner decision 2026-10-29).** Because nobody reviews the script, the gate is code: (a) a back-translation faithfulness check (Gemma translates each beat to English; a second pass lists facts in the beat that are missing from or contradicted by the Material Pack); (b) a naturalness judge pass with a fixed rubric (stiff Hindi, English word stuffing, grammar slips, gender agreement with the first-person male narrator); (c) the rule checks (banned phrases, question budget, romanised Hindi, digits, mixed script); (d) a hard stop in auto-run when any of them fails twice. Residual risk, accepted by the owner: subtle Hindi errors and tone misses can still reach a video.

## 13. Second sweep (2026-10-29): findings that change the plan

A second pass over audio, all stored thumbnails, the book-based film, the story pool and the auto-run wiring found eight more things. Each is now a task in [TASKS_quality_overhaul.md](TASKS_quality_overhaul.md).

1. **Audio is quiet and full of dead air.** Voice takes of the newest film measure -25.8 LUFS integrated (peak -9 dBFS); the finished video measures -25.6 LUFS. YouTube normalises loud audio down but does not lift quiet audio, so these videos play noticeably quieter than the rest of the feed (common target is about -14 LUFS, true peak about -1 dBTP). Silence below -38 dB lasting at least 0.3 s makes up **23 to 38% of every voice take** (38% in the 104-second beat), although the plan's own pauses sum to only a few seconds per beat. The engine adds a gap after nearly every sentence. A 1.25x speed-up keeps that dead air and shortens only the speech. Trimming gaps to 0.25 to 0.4 s except planned pauses should shorten an 8.5-minute film by roughly a minute and a half with no loss of content.
2. **Thumbnails have no system.** Four films, four designs. The three older ones are very dark, a face in shadow or a silhouette with a headline ("Arrested without moving?", "How did 18-year-old Rohit steal 64.38 crore?", "64.38 crore fraud"), low contrast at phone size, and an unlocalised number ("64.38 crore" instead of "₹64 करोड़"). The newest is bright but shows the wrong person and invented map text. No thumbnail has been tested at 170 by 95 pixels.
3. **Silent fallback from edit to plain drawing.** The newest film's setting says scene pictures use `edit` mode with Qwen, but it has **no reference pictures at all** and all 73 pictures were drawn plain with zero references (`mode: generate`, `references: 0`). `scenes.resolve_mode` quietly falls back when no approved reference exists, and auto-run reaches the pictures stage without making them. The earlier Surat and dark-matter films did use 1 to 3 references per picture. So the "faces change between scenes" problem in the newest film is partly a wiring gap: the setting promises something that did not happen and nothing says so.
4. **The finance explainer film shows the failure mode of non-incident stories.** The 1987-crash film (older game-art direction) places figures on floating blue grid platforms, whirlpools, a sprouting plant creature, dominoes on a table, coins, and captions such as "Shock Spiral" and "Domino Effect". Its place list is "the abstract market grid". The current `filmic` default avoids most of it, but explainers and listicles have no visual grammar of their own: they need grounded analogies from Indian daily life (a kirana ledger, a mandi, a local train at rush hour, a chai stall queue) plus built charts, not abstract worlds.
5. **Phase 1 splits one article into several competing stories and titles are internal.** The fugitive article produced four candidates ("The 35-Year Fugitive", "The Power of a 14-Year-Old Photo", "The Identity Shift of Chhabbi Lal", "The Meat Cleaver Murder of 1991"). The approved one is the broadest; the most vivid details sit in the others and are lost. Candidate titles are labels ("The Anatomy of a Trading Failure"), not YouTube titles.
6. **A title can contradict the facts it summarises.** The book film "The Knight Capital Nightmare" is built on a January 2007 17% earnings-day drop, while, to my knowledge, Knight Capital is best known for an August 2012 trading-software failure. A viewer who clicks on the famous name gets a different story. The pipeline has no check that a title or thumbnail claim is something the video delivers.
7. **Approval is self-graded.** Auto-run approves content and sign-off with warnings, then continues (`story3/auto.py` stops only on sign-off blockers, missing models and missing media). No threshold stops a weak film.
8. **Nothing derives short-form content.** In 2026 discovery leans heavily on Shorts. The Material Pack and event ledger will already hold the hook moments, so cutting 3 to 5 vertical teasers per film is cheap once they exist (kept as a later milestone; the roadmap already lists a 9:16 plan).
9. **Cost of the new design.** Three-pass narration, Material Pack, event ledger and judge passes add about 15 to 25 minutes of chat time per film. Pictures stay the main cost (about 95 minutes). Dead-air trimming and a scene cap change picture count: a mean of 4.5 to 5.5 s per scene means 90 to 110 scenes for 8 minutes instead of 59, which would add 35 to 60 minutes of picture time. The plan therefore draws a *second angle of the same event through edit mode* and uses parallax and Wan clips for part of the extra shots; this is why V7 and the scene-count budget exist in the task list.

### 14.1 Viewer simulation of the newest film (`pl-0b7cead5`)

Method. I read the narration in order, looked at the pictures at each moment, and measured two curves per 30 seconds: the **share of new words** (words not yet spoken in the film) and the **share of scenes that introduce a new place or subject**. Times are narration time before the 1.25x speed-up.

| Narration time | What the viewer gets | Hook state |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:14 | A man looks at a transformer; a shout "Chabbi Lal!"; he turns. One strong, concrete beat. | Hooked |
| 0:14 to 1:01 | "मैं आपको उस पल की गंभीरता महसूस कराना चाहता हूँ", "यह सिर्फ एक simple arrest नहीं है", two rhetorical questions. The arrest is already shown, the promise is vague. | **First drop risk** (about 0:30 to 0:45) |
| 1:01 to 2:45 | The 1991 murder told without an image of what happened, five questions, 24 scenes in one house with the same three faces. No detail about the victims as people. | **Drop risk** unless the viewer already cares |
| 2:45 to 4:25 | "Kolkata, Mumbai, Nagpur, Goa" one sentence each, then abstract lines about shadows and new faces. New-word share falls from 63% to 41%. | **Highest drop risk** (about 3:30) |
| 4:25 to 5:47 | "Proclaimed offender", "caretaker is present but unseen", "interesting job". New words 37%. | Drop risk |
| 5:47 to 7:05 | The answer the whole film promised (how the police found him) arrives as "a number and a blurred photo" with a rhetorical question that is never answered; police scenes sit in the victim's house. | Frustration |
| 7:05 to 8:30 | The capture, finally concrete (a team, a shout). Ends on an English teaser line. | Payoff arrives too late and too thin |

Curves (newest film, new-word share per 30 s from the start): 97, 87, 76, 63, 53, 60, 63, 41, 37, 58, 44, 34, 52, 29, 29, 43, 33 per cent. The Surat film falls to 22% in its last 30 s, the digital-arrest film to 35 to 38% in the middle. **Information density halves by the middle of every film** and the last third recycles earlier vocabulary. New place or subject per 30 s: the newest film shows 0 to 1 new items per window from minute 3:30 to 6:30 (4:00: 1 of 3, 4:30: 1 of 2, 6:30: 0 of 4); the Surat film has 0 new items in six windows of its second half.

**What the viewer pattern says.** The film spends its only vivid moment in the first 14 seconds, then asks the viewer to wait seven minutes through general statements for the part the story is really about (the discovery chain: family calls, a dozen numbers checked, a keypad phone, a six-hour watch, the shout). A viewer who is hooked by "Hidden for 35 Years?" is let down at about 0:45 and again at about 3:30.

### 14.2 What would keep me watching to the end (the hook stack)

As a viewer of an Indian crime-and-explainer channel in 2026, these are the things that make me stay. Each is turned into a build requirement.

1. **A stake that touches me.** "Yeh mere saath bhi ho sakta hai" for scam stories; "ek aam aadmi 35 saal tak aapke mohalle mein chhupa raha" for chase stories. The cold open must say who is at risk and why I should care within 15 seconds.
2. **A fair mystery I can play.** Show the clue *before* the explanation (a call-log card with one repeating number; a blurred photo; a keypad phone) so I can guess. Then answer it. The current films ask me to guess and never answer.
3. **A clock or counter.** "Six hours", "one number, one photo", "121 apps, 21,672 phones". Concrete constraints create tension; the source has them and the script leaves them out.
4. **A turn every 60 to 90 seconds.** A reversal or a new fact, not a rhetorical question: he thinks both victims are dead, one is not; the break comes from calling his own family; he is caught by his own name.
5. **A micro-reward every 20 to 30 seconds.** A new fact, person, place, object or number. Measured target: at least 45% new words per 30 s until the last minute, and a new place or subject at least once in every other 30-second window.
6. **One set piece.** A cinematic sequence of 40 to 60 seconds that is the film's showpiece (the six-hour stakeout told shot by shot with the clock; the app that reads OTPs live). Everything else builds to it.
7. **Humanity and irony in small details.** He kept the phone off; he was paid Rs 15,000 a month and lived in a terrace room; his wife told police he never came back; the son is now married with a one-year-old. These make people feel, share and comment.
8. **An ending that reframes.** The house where it happened has been sold and split into four homes. One closing image and one line that stays.
9. **A reason to comment.** One honest question at the end ("आपके हिसाब से Montu को कैसा लगा होगा?"), plus one "pause and guess" moment in the middle.
10. **Sound that carries mood.** A pulse during the stakeout, silence before the shout, a clock tick for a countdown (currently there is no sound design).

### 14.3 Making each film a distinct experience (without a template feel)

The goal is a recognisable channel with films that do not feel like the same video twice. Two layers: a **fixed channel frame** (logo, thumbnail border, caption face, intro length) and a **per-film experience design** the pipeline writes and then follows.

**Experience design per film** (new `blueprint.experience`, task 2.14): `promise`, `stake_to_viewer`, `clock_device` (a countdown, a counter, or none), `clue_device` (how the viewer plays detective), `set_piece` (which beat and what it shows), `participation` (the pause-and-guess moment and the closing question), `afterimage` (the last image), `signature_graphic` (the built card type that recurs in this film: call log, chat bubbles, FIR scrap, newspaper cutting, route map, ledger).

**Tools that make films feel produced, not slideshows** (all built by code or from existing models):
- **Evidence cards built by code** (task 3.15): a generic chat-app thread (no brand, no logo), a bank SMS, a call log with one repeating number, an FIR page scrap, a newspaper clipping with a headline, a phone-screen point of view, an ID card, a ledger, a route map that draws itself. These carry the facts the pictures cannot, and they are the best fix for "illustrating words".
- **Era grading and era accuracy** (task 3.13): past scenes warm and grainy, present scenes cleaner and cooler, with an era table that blocks wrong objects (a smartphone in 1991; a smartphone where the story is a keypad phone).
- **Act-level camera language** (task 3.14): act one locked and calm, act two handheld drift and closer, act three slow pushes; point-of-view shots through objects (a phone screen, a window, a CCTV frame).
- **Progress devices on screen** (task 5.9): chapter title flash for two seconds, a clue counter, a countdown, a day counter, chosen per film.
- **A different story-shape per film** (task 7.6): mystery, countdown, two-timeline, case-file, "what would you do" for scam films; never the same opening form twice in a row.
- **Participation moments** (task 5.9): "pause and guess" (a held still with a ticking sound, 3 seconds) once, and a real closing question.

### 14.4 Avoiding repetitive people and the tiny world

Measured today: 2 to 6 places per film, one protagonist in 30 to 50% of scenes, 63 to 85% of scenes with a person, one face for seven officers.

**World rules (each is a measurable check; tasks 3.2, 3.11, 3.12):**
- **Face-free share at least 45% of scenes.** Types that count: place wide, object insert, hands, a person from behind or in silhouette, document card, map, phone screen, texture, a crowd seen at distance.
- **Protagonist at most 35% of scenes and never more than two close-ups in a row**; a new person or a new place enters in every beat.
- **Cast of at least eight distinct people for a six-minute film**, each with a different silhouette (age, build, clothing colour, a signature item). Officers are two or three recurring individuals plus extras, not one face.
- **Places of different kinds**: home, street, institution, transit, workplace, nature, night, interior and exterior. At least 12 places for six minutes, at least two per beat.
- **The world around the people** (task 3.12): 8 to 12 short inserts per film of ordinary Indian life that belong to the story's region and time: a tea stall, a platform, a power transformer humming, wall posters, a scooter horn, hanging laundry, rain on a tin roof, a temple bell, school children. Each is a 1.5 to 2.5 second cutaway drawn per film with the region's cues (see 14.5). They make the world feel big and cost little.
- **Scale and time on screen**: a route map for travel, a calendar or age change for years, a counter for numbers. A 35-year story must show 35 years.
- **Motif budget**: one recurring object (the transformer) at most four times, planted and paid off, not nine.

**Cultural fit issues found in the data (not covered in sections 3 to 13):**
- **Names that do not match the cast.** In the dark-matter film the lead is "Sam Eriksen" (a Scandinavian-style name) drawn as an Indian woman because the Indian-cast rule fixes the look but not the name. Names, clothing and places must agree (task 3.16).
- **Regional cue coverage is nearly zero.** Counting region-specific cues in the scene prompts of the newest film: kurta in 26 of 59 prompts, saree in 5, rickshaw in 3, tram and taxi in 3 each; no turban, dupatta, regional signage, dhaba, auto, scooter, tractor or festival marker in a Punjab story. Every man wears a cream kurta because the registry gives one outfit per role.
- **Era errors.** 1991 Delhi shown as a rural courtyard; a smartphone in an officer's hand in a film whose clue is a keypad phone; the 18-year-old son drawn as a child; the 62-year-old drawn at about 38.
- **Representation of victims.** A smiling victim; named private persons; the surviving son named.
- **Language register.** English numerals inside Hindi sentences; stiff Hindi ("कातिल बन गया है", "खामोश मंजर") beside slangy English ("narrow down", "interesting"); phrases that a Hindi-first viewer finds tiresome ("सोचिए" five times).
- **Device and data.** Most viewers watch on a phone, often on mobile data and often with sound off or in noise. Thin captions, dark thumbnails (three of four stored thumbnails are very dark) and quiet audio (-25.8 LUFS) all hurt on a phone. Encode a lighter 720p version for low-data viewers (task 5.6).
- **Sensitive-topic handling.** Digital-arrest and app-fraud videos should always show the real helpline and a short "what to do now"; violent-crime reconstructions need implied-only treatment (owner decision).
